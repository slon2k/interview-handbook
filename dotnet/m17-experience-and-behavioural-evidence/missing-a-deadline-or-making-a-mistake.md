# Missing a Deadline or Making a Mistake

## What Interviewers Are Listening For

This is one of the more heavily-weighted behavioral categories, precisely because how someone handles their own failure is hard to fake and highly predictive of how they'll behave on a real team. Interviewers are listening for genuine ownership (not self-flagellation, not deflection) and a concrete change afterward.

## Prompts to Find Your Real Story

- Did you underestimate how long something would take, and how did you communicate that once you realized it?
- Did you ship a bug that reached production, and how did you handle the aftermath — separate from the technical fix (which is a different scenario category) — how did you communicate it to your team or manager?
- Did you make an estimate you now know was wrong, and what specifically would you estimate differently next time?
- Is there a mistake that, in hindsight, you're honestly a little embarrassed by — these often make the most credible stories, since a story with a mistake that doesn't actually feel costly to admit can come across as chosen for safety rather than honesty.

## A Generic Shape (Template Only — Fill In Your Actual Details)

```
Situation: [what the deadline or task was, and what went wrong, specifically]
Task:      [what you were responsible for]
Action:    [when you realized there was a problem, and what you did the MOMENT you
            realized it — did you flag it immediately, or sit on it hoping to catch up?]
Result:    [how it actually played out, what the impact was, and — critically — what you
            changed about how you estimate/communicate/work as a direct result]
```

## Common Weak Answers

- Choosing a "mistake" that isn't really a mistake (a humble-brag disguised as a flaw) — interviewers can usually tell, and it undermines the honesty the question is trying to assess.
- Excessive self-criticism with no forward-looking resolution, which can read as a lack of confidence rather than genuine accountability.
- Deflecting responsibility onto unclear requirements, a difficult teammate, or bad luck, rather than owning your specific part in it.
- No concrete change afterward — if nothing about how you work actually changed, that's a genuine gap worth being honest about rather than manufacturing a lesson that doesn't feel real.

## Follow-up Questions to Expect

- "What was the moment you realized there was a problem?"
- "How did you communicate it, and to whom, and how soon?"
- "What do you do differently now because of this?"
