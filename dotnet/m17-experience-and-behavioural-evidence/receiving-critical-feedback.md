# Receiving Critical Feedback

## What Interviewers Are Listening For

Whether you can hear something you didn't want to hear without getting defensive, and whether you actually changed anything as a result — as opposed to just saying "I took it well" with no evidence. This is the receiving-side counterpart to Module 15's code-review content, which specifically discussed responding to feedback collaboratively rather than defensively.

## Prompts to Find Your Real Story

- Did a reviewer catch something you'd genuinely missed — a bug, a security gap (Module 12), a design issue (Module 4/14) — and how did you actually react in the moment, not just in retrospect?
- Did you receive feedback that stung a little because it was accurate? (These often make more credible stories than feedback you disagreed with, since agreeing to change is a stronger signal than being persuaded.)
- Did feedback from one review change how you approached your *next* piece of work, not just the one being reviewed?
- Was there feedback you initially resisted but came to agree with later?

## A Generic Shape (Template Only — Fill In Your Actual Details)

```
Situation: [what the feedback was, specifically — and don't soften it; specific, slightly
            uncomfortable feedback makes a much more credible story than vague feedback]
Task:      [what you did with it immediately — did you push back reflexively, or take a
            moment before responding?]
Action:    [what you actually changed as a direct result]
Result:    [how that change played out afterward, and ideally how it affected later work,
            not just the one instance]
```

## Common Weak Answers

- A story with no real sting to the feedback — "someone suggested a minor improvement and I made it" doesn't demonstrate handling anything genuinely critical.
- Claiming you "took it well" with no description of an actual initial reaction, which often reads as glossing over a real (and completely normal) moment of defensiveness.
- No lasting change — if the feedback didn't affect anything beyond the one instance, that's worth being honest about rather than overstating its impact.
- Undermining the story by subtly implying the feedback was actually wrong or unfair, which contradicts the framing of having "received" it well.

## Follow-up Questions to Expect

- "What was your honest first reaction?"
- "Did you agree with the feedback right away, or did it take time?"
- "Has that feedback changed how you approach similar situations since?"
